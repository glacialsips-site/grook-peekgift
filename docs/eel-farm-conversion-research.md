# Trout-Farm-to-Eel-Farm Conversion — Preliminary Research Foundation

**Purpose:** Factual foundation for a business plan to convert an existing gravity-fed, cold-water flow-through trout farm into an American eel (*Anguilla rostrata*) grow-out operation in the central US East Coast region (VA / MD / NC / WV area — flexible).

**Status:** Preliminary. This is a fact-finding step, not a plan. Confidence levels and open questions are flagged throughout so the business plan can weight each input appropriately.

**Method:** Multi-source web research with adversarial verification (25 sources fetched, 105 candidate claims extracted, 25 highest-value claims triple-verified; 24 confirmed, 1 refuted). Species-transfer caveat applies throughout — see [§8](#8-caveats-and-how-to-read-this).

---

## TL;DR — the two things that decide this business

1. **Temperature is a design problem, not a blocker.** Eels grow fastest at ~22.5–26.5 °C (preferred 23–28 °C). Your cold spring/stream inflow (~15–16 °C) simply means slower growth unless warmed — and warming is exactly what a **partial-recirculation** loop buys you (retain and reuse heat instead of dumping it downstream). You've already said you're not worried about sub-optimal temps; the research supports that framing — cold water costs *growth rate*, not *viability*.

2. **Legal juvenile supply is the binding constraint.** Harvesting or possessing glass eels/elvers (the under-9-inch juveniles you'd stock) is **prohibited in every Atlantic state except Maine and South Carolina**. Total legal domestic supply is tiny (Maine quota 9,688 lb/yr; SC <750 lb/yr) and elvers sell to East Asian buyers for **>$2,000/lb**. This — not biology or engineering — is why US eel grow-out is rare, and it's the single biggest item to nail down before anything else. **You said you'll handle sourcing "if it's legal"; the legality of the *interstate purchase/transport pathway* is the open question that most needs a real answer.**

## 1. Biology & culture requirements

| Parameter | Value | Confidence | Source |

| Temperature optimum | 22.5–26.5 °C (peer-reviewed); preferred 23–28 °C (industry) | High | PMC12382684; Business Queensland |
| Time to market (150–200 g) | 12–18 months (industry); 18–24 mo in other refs | High / Med | Business Queensland; secondary |

| Dissolved oxygen | Floor ~3 ppm (keep well above; optima higher) | High | Business Queensland |
| Free (un-ionized) ammonia | < 0.2 mg/L | High | Business Queensland |
| Growth variability | **Extreme** — eels grade themselves into fast/slow cohorts; frequent size-grading is standard practice | High | Business Queensland |

**Temperature ↔ growth (the number that drives your pro-forma).** A peer-reviewed Japanese-eel study explicitly demonstrated the cold-water-farm scenario: it heated 15–16 °C inflow up to ~24.6 °C in a flow-through system with a 2 kW heater, and held a RAS at 25 °C. This is a direct precedent for warming cold source water toward the optimum. Model growth as a function of realized tank temperature, not source temperature — your recirc/heating strategy sets the difference.

**Salinity is a free growth lever (optional).** Saltwater-resident American eels grow **~2.2× faster in length and ~5.3× faster in weight** than freshwater residents. Brackish/salt rearing "could improve growth rates in eel aquaculture." *Caveat:* this is observational field data on wild eels (salinity confounded with temperature and food), not a controlled farm trial, and plenty of farms run freshwater successfully. Treat it as an upside option to explore (you have a freshwater stream, so salt would mean added infrastructure), not a requirement. **Confidence: high on the effect, medium on the magnitude transferring to a tank.**

**Water-quality envelope is forgiving.** European-eel elvers survived 60 days at 77.8% at DO 7.47 mg/L, pH 8.06, TAN 1.31 mg/L (un-ionized NH₃ ~0.039 mg/L), nitrite 0.046 mg/L — comfortably inside the guideline envelope. Eels are hardy relative to trout on ammonia tolerance.

**Grading is a real operating cost.** Growth is famously heterogeneous within a cohort; expect to grade repeatedly and run multiple size classes in parallel. Budget labor and tank count for this.

> **Refuted — do not use:** A claim that tank biomass "should not exceed 50 kg/m³ and ponds 10 t/ha" failed verification (0–3). Other sources cite commercial densities of 50–150 kg/m³, and a giant-mottled-eel aquaponic trial found best FCR at ~11.5 kg/m³ — i.e. **the literature does not give one clean stocking-density number.** Treat stocking density as a to-be-determined engineering parameter, not a settled figure.

## 2. Trout-farm → eel conversion

**The core good news:** *culture-system type does not itself drive eel growth — feed type does.* A controlled study found RAS vs flow-through had **no significant effect** on Japanese-eel growth (p > 0.05), while feed type was significant. **Your existing flow-through raceway infrastructure is not an inherent growth handicap versus a purpose-built RAS.** That materially de-risks the "convert vs build new" decision.

What a trout raceway already gives you that transfers directly:
- Gravity-fed water delivery, head, and channel structure
- Settling/effluent handling basics
- Solids management and screening know-how

What has to change for eels (from the culture requirements, applied to raceway realities):
- **Fine screening** everywhere water enters/leaves — glass eels/elvers are tiny and will pass trout-scale screens.
- **Escape prevention / cover.** Eels climb and squeeze; every tank lip, standpipe, and outlet needs eel-tight covering and sealing. This is a well-known, non-trivial containment task.
- **Heating / heat retention** to move ~15–16 °C source water toward the 22.5–26.5 °C optimum (see §3 — this is where recirc pays off).
- **Aeration** sized to warmer water (DO capacity falls as temperature rises) and to eel densities.
- **Tank geometry** favoring eel behavior (they're benthic, thigmotactic — like structure/hides), vs long trout raceways.

**Documented salmonid-to-eel conversions:** not directly found in surviving sources. The *component* precedents are strong (Danish Model Trout Farm recirc designs, eel RAS engineering), but a clean "we converted a trout farm to eels" case study is an **open item** worth a targeted search / operator interviews.

## 3. Flow-through + flow control + partial recirculation

This is the part of your concept the research most directly validates.

**Partial recirculation is an established eel-culture design lever.** Heinsbroek & Kamstra (*Aquacultural Engineering*, 1990) — a paper purpose-built on eel recirc systems — found performance improves by **increasing tank flow rates** and **optimizing the combination of biofilter flow rate, system volume, and biofilter surface area.** That's precisely the knob-set your "flow control + variable recirc" concept is built around.

**Why partial recirc, specifically for you:**
- **Warming:** recirculating culture water retains metabolic + heater heat instead of flushing it to the stream — the cheapest path to raising realized tank temperature. (Directly demonstrated in the PMC12382684 heating example.)
- **Water conservation:** a Danish Model Trout Farm–style recirc ran make-up water at **9 m³ per kg of fish produced** — a dramatic cut vs classical flow-through. General RAS practice replaces **5–10% of system volume/day** with new water to hold nitrate/organics down (reducible when water is scarce, with a full exchange between cycles).
- **Ammonia control:** biofiltration in the recirc loop handles TAN so you're not relying solely on dilution.

**Your blend concept (fresh stream / recirculated culture water / settling-pond effluent, any adjustable ratio)** is consistent with best practice — it's essentially a tunable make-up-water strategy layered on a recirc loop. The 5–10%/day exchange and 9 m³/kg figures give you starting anchors for the blend-ratio math when you design plumbing later.

**Micro-hydro at the intake to power recirc pumps:** conceptually plausible (gravity head + continuous flow is the ideal micro-hydro case), but **no source directly addressed powering recirc pumps with intake micro-hydro**, and it carries **water-rights + FERC/state licensing** questions. Flag as promising-but-unverified; feasibility hinges on your actual head/flow and local water law. **Confidence: low (concept only).**

## 4. Sourcing juveniles — the legal reality

**This is the make-or-break section.**

- **Harvest/possession of glass eels & elvers (< 9 inches) is prohibited in every Atlantic state except Maine and South Carolina.** The ASMFC Interstate Fishery Management Plan sets a **9-inch commercial and recreational minimum size**, so sub-9-inch juveniles cannot legally be taken or possessed outside those two managed fisheries. (Florida is technically a third state on paper but has no fishable elver population.)
- **Maine:** managed fishery, **9,688 lb annual quota** (set for 2025–2027, originating in Addendum IV; not exceeded since 2016), **425-license cap**, restricted gear (hand dip nets, fyke nets, Sheldon eel traps).
- **South Carolina:** small fishery, **< 750 lb/yr**.
- **Total legal domestic elver supply is therefore ~10,000 lb/yr**, essentially all spoken for and priced at **>$2,000/lb** for export.

**What the sources did *not* establish (critical open questions):**
- Whether a VA/MD/NC/WV grow-out farm can **legally purchase and transport Maine/SC elvers interstate** for stocking under the ASMFC FMP + state aquaculture-stocking/importation permits.
- Whether the practical legal path is instead to **stock domestically-produced yellow eels above 9 inches** (legal to harvest in many states) and grow those out — which sidesteps the elver bottleneck entirely but changes your input size, cost, and growth timeline.
- **Importation** rules (e.g. from foreign glass-eel sources) — not confirmed, and likely CITES/Lacey-Act-sensitive.

**Recommendation for the plan:** treat "legal, permitted juvenile supply chain" as **Gate #0** — the assumption every downstream number depends on. Resolve it with a fisheries attorney + direct calls to ASMFC, Maine DMR, and the target state's aquaculture office *before* committing capital. Your stance ("I'll find the fish if it's legal") is right; the research says *legal* is the hard part, and the yellow-eel-above-9-inch route may be the cleaner legal entry.

## 5. Regulatory / production thresholds

**The ~500,000 lb figure was not found as a defined regulatory tier.** The load-bearing federal numbers are different and lower:

- **100,000 lb/yr — federal EPA CAAP effluent-guideline / NPDES trigger** (40 CFR Part 451, 2004 final rule). CAAP effluent guidelines apply to facilities that: produce **≥ 100,000 lb/yr**, use flow-through / recirculating / net-pen systems, **AND directly discharge** wastewater. Hit all three and you're subject to the CAAP effluent guidelines within your NPDES permit.
- **40 CFR Part 122 App. C exclusions:** **warm-water** facilities < **100,000 lb/yr** and **cold-water** facilities < **20,000 lb/yr** (harvest weight) are excluded from *mandatory* CAAP designation (the permitting authority keeps case-by-case discretion).

**What this means for you:**
- If you **directly discharge** to the stream and scale up, you'll likely trigger **CAAP effluent guidelines + NPDES permitting** with **settling/solids controls**. Your settling-pond concept is directly on point — solids capture is exactly what these rules target.
- **Sub-threshold does not mean permit-free:** smaller/direct-discharge facilities still generally need an **NPDES permit**; they're only exempt from the *specific CAAP effluent guidelines*.
- Whether eels count as "warm-water" or "cold-water" for the threshold (100k vs 20k) is worth confirming — you'd farm a warm-water-optimum species in a cold-water-sourced system, an ambiguity worth a direct question to the permitting authority.
- **Recirculation reduces discharge volume**, which can help you stay under thresholds or shrink permit burden — another point in favor of your partial-recirc design.

**Open item:** state-level aquaculture facility, water-withdrawal, and NPDES/settling-pond specifics for **VA / MD / NC / WV** were **not** confirmed and vary by state — each has its own aquaculture office and discharge program. This needs per-state research once you narrow the site.

## 6. Market & economics

**Confirmed:**
- The **juvenile (elver) trade** is the high-value chokepoint: **>$2,000/lb** to East Asian buyers; Maine's 2012–2013 landings were valued at **$30–40M+**. East Asian eel aquaculture (Japan, China, Taiwan, Korea — the unagi/kabayaki market) depends **entirely on wild-caught glass-eel fry**, because eels are not reliably bred in captivity at commercial scale.
- **Why US grow-out is uncommon:** restricted, extremely valuable juvenile supply — the elvers are worth more sold to Asia than grown out domestically — plus the sourcing legality maze. As of 2023 there were only ~5 US eel farms (four FL, one ME), down from 7 in 2018.

**Not confirmed (open questions for the plan):**
- **Grow-out (adult, 150–200 g+) market price per pound** and demand volumes for live export to Asia and US/EU markets — the *revenue* side of your pro-forma is the least-verified number here.
- **Competitor RAS economics** (China, Netherlands, Denmark) — capex/opex/breakeven — not verified.
- The strategic tension to resolve: is the money in **selling adults** (grow-out, your plan) or is the market structure such that **juveniles capture most of the value**? The plan should stress-test adult price assumptions hard.

## 8. Caveats and how to read this

- **Species transfer:** Most husbandry parameters (water quality, temperature optimum, FCR, growth timeline, system-vs-feed finding) come from **Australian (*A. australis/reinhardtii*), Japanese (*A. japonica*), or European (*A. anguilla*)** eels — congeners of American eel (*A. rostrata*). Figures generalize across *Anguilla* but are **not rostrata-specific**; the plan should flag this and, where a number is load-bearing, seek an *A. rostrata* confirmation.
- **Salinity growth benefit** is observational wild-eel field data (salinity confounded with temperature/food), not a controlled aquaculture trial.
- **Refuted claim** (do not use): the "50 kg/m³ tank / 10 t/ha pond" stocking-density figure failed verification. Stocking density is unsettled in the literature — treat as an engineering unknown.
- **Time-sensitivity:** elver quotas and prices move on multi-year cycles (Maine 9,688 lb for 2025–2027) and are volatile — re-verify current ASMFC addenda and Maine DMR figures at plan time.
- **The 500,000 lb figure** in the original brief was **not** located as a defined tier; the real federal thresholds are **100,000 lb (warm-water CAAP)** and **20,000 lb (cold-water CAAP designation)**. If "500k lb" came from a specific state program, that source should be identified directly.
- **Not confirmed by this pass:** interstate juvenile purchase/transport legality, adult market price/demand, competitor RAS economics, and state-level permitting for VA/MD/NC/WV.
